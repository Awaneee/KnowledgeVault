## PART 1 — SYSTEM HEALTH

Every metric measured by the evaluation suite, graded and contextualized.

---

### 1.1 Retrieval — MRR@5 (Semantic)

**What it measures:** The reciprocal rank of the first relevant document. MRR=1.0 means the right note is always ranked first. MRR=0.5 means the first relevant note appears at rank 2 on average.

**Measured value:** 0.553 ± 0.424 (std dev)

**Grade: Medium**

**Industry context:** Production RAG systems on well-formed corpora typically achieve MRR 0.65–0.85 on curated benchmarks. Personal knowledge management is harder — queries are vague, the corpus is heterogeneous, and there is no query log to tune against. 0.553 on a 1055-note, 152-query benchmark is acceptable but not strong.

**Important qualifier:** The 95% confidence interval on this MRR is ±0.067. The measurement is statistically noisy. The 152-query benchmark has insufficient power to detect MRR differences below 0.10.

**Is this limiting the system?** Yes, but not the primary bottleneck. The real problem is that 39 of 152 queries (25.7%) return MRR=0.0 — the correct note is not in the top 5 at all. The aggregate MRR hides a bimodal distribution: queries that succeed tend to rank the right note at position 1 (contributing 1.0 to MRR), while failures contribute 0.0.

---

### 1.2 Retrieval — MRR@5 (Hybrid vs Semantic)

**Measured values:** Semantic = 0.553, Hybrid = 0.587. ΔMRR = +0.034.

**Grade: Poor (the hybrid contribution, not the absolute value)**

**The critical finding:** ΔMRR of +0.034 is smaller than the 95% confidence interval (±0.067). To detect a ΔMRR of 0.05 at 80% statistical power requires 443 queries. The current benchmark has 152. The hybrid improvement is unmeasurably small.

**What this means in practice:** When the system runs a query, the user experiences no detectable benefit from the 98ms of additional work the intent arm performs. On the original 50-query benchmark, ΔMRR was exactly 0.000.

---

### 1.3 Retrieval — Hit Rate@5

**What it measures:** Fraction of queries where at least one relevant note appears in the top 5.

**Measured value:** 74.3% (Semantic and Hybrid identical)

**Grade: Acceptable**

**Context:** For a 1055-note personal corpus with heterogeneous topics and imperfect benchmark ground truth, 74.3% hit rate is acceptable. The 25.7% miss rate splits into two causes:
- Genuine retrieval failures (query semantics don't match note embeddings)
- Benchmark ground truth failures (expected notes include off-topic notes that happen to contain query keywords)

**Is this limiting the system?** Moderately. The 39 zero-hit queries include both real failures and benchmark artifacts. Resolving which is which requires corpus deduplication and benchmark ground truth cleanup first.

---

### 1.4 Retrieval — Recall@5 (Semantic)

**What it measures:** Of all expected relevant notes, what fraction appear in the top 5.

**Measured value:** 0.208

**Grade: Poor — but partially a benchmark measurement artifact**

**Critical context:** Recall@5 = 0.208 means on average only 20.8% of expected notes appear in the top 5. This sounds alarming. But the expected sets were generated by keyword matching — any note containing a keyword is considered relevant. For "Python programming notes," the expected set includes note 272 ("Career advice: specialize in one area — my T: backend Python/FastAPI"), a career advice note that contains "Python" as a skill, not as subject matter.

The factual lookup category — where expected notes are carefully selected (1–3 specific notes) — achieves Hit Rate = 100% (20/20 queries) and Recall = 0.367, which is reasonable for K=5 returning 5 of the ~8 truly relevant notes.

The 0.208 aggregate is pulled down by synthesis and comparison queries with large expected sets (up to 10 notes) that are themselves unreliable ground truth.

**Is this limiting the system?** The benchmark is not a reliable instrument for Recall below query types that have specific ground truth. Fix the corpus and benchmark before acting on this number.

---

### 1.5 Retrieval — Precision@5

**What it measures:** Of the 5 retrieved notes, what fraction are in the expected set.

**Measured value:** 0.307 (Semantic), 0.308 (Hybrid)

**Grade: Acceptable**

When the system retrieves something, roughly 1 in 3 of those results is in the expected set. For a heterogeneous 1055-note personal corpus, this is adequate. The system is not returning obviously irrelevant results — the precision failure is that it retrieves notes about related topics rather than exactly the queried topic.

---

### 1.6 Retrieval — Latency

**Measured values:**
- Semantic: **1.3ms avg, 4.7ms p95**
- Intent arm only: 28.6ms
- Hybrid (full): **98.7ms avg, 98.5ms p95**

**Grades:**
- Semantic: **Excellent** — sub-2ms retrieval on 1055 notes is fast
- Hybrid overhead: **Poor** — +97ms (+7,500%) for ΔMRR = +0.034

**The latency math:** The intent arm costs 97ms and produces an MRR gain of 0.034 (if real). At this rate, reducing latency from 98.7ms to 1.3ms (by removing the intent arm) would cost only 0.034 MRR — a trade most production systems would make without hesitation.

**Important note:** The 1.3ms semantic latency is a sequential KNN scan (O(N)). There is **no HNSW or IVFFlat index** on any of the three vector tables. At 1055 notes this is fine. At 10,000 notes this becomes 10ms. At 100,000 notes it becomes 100ms. This is a latency timebomb that does not yet hurt but is waiting to explode.

---

### 1.7 Intent Accuracy

**What it measures:** Fraction of queries where the fast keyword classifier assigns the correct intent type (study, todo, reminder, etc.).

**Measured values:**
- 50-query benchmark (original dataset): 34.0%
- 152-query benchmark (new dataset): **14.5%**

**Grade: Critical**

**Industry context:** A 9-class intent classifier that achieves 14.5% accuracy is worse than a classifier that always predicts the most common class. The most common predicted class is "general" (19/152 queries), which baseline accuracy would give ~12.5%. The fast classifier is barely above random and gets worse as query diversity increases.

**Why it degraded from 34% to 14.5%:** The original 50-query benchmark was designed specifically for the original 54-note corpus and was not representative of natural query diversity. The 152-query benchmark includes synthesis, comparison, and ambiguous queries that users naturally phrase as "what...", "how...", "why..." — all of which the classifier routes to "question" or "general" rather than "study" or "reference."

**Is this limiting the system?** Yes — this is the primary reason the intent arm fails. Every metric downstream of intent classification is capped by 14.5%.

---

### 1.8 Intent Hit Rate

**What it measures:** Fraction of queries where the intent arm retrieves at least one expected relevant note.

**Measured value:** **1.3% (2/152 queries)**

**Grade: Critical — effectively broken**

Intent retrieval works on 2 out of 152 queries. The entire intent-based retrieval arm is delivering essentially no value. This is the most damaging single metric in the system.

---

### 1.9 Category Reuse — Genuine vs Forced

**Measured breakdown of 1055 assignment methods:**
- `canonical_name`: 313 (29.7%) — exact category name match, genuine reuse
- `created`: 45 (4.3%) — new category, genuine creation
- `exact_rule`: 29 (2.7%) — rule-based match, genuine
- `cap_fallback`: **668 (63.3%) — forced into nearest category after cap hit**

**Grade: Critical**

The reported "fragmentation rate = 0%" and "reuse rate = 100%" are misleading. The genuine reuse rate is 29.7% + 2.7% = 32.4%. The remaining 63.3% of assignments are not based on semantic compatibility — they are forced by the 50-category cap into whatever existing category happens to be nearest in embedding space.

**The cap was hit at note 155** — batch 1 of 10. Every note ingested after the 155th was potentially misassigned.

---

### 1.10 Category Purity

**What it measures:** Are notes in a category semantically related to each other?

**Grade: Poor for large categories**

The `Tasks` category holds 242 notes (23% of the corpus). It was designed for todo items but absorbed everything that had nowhere else to go after the cap was hit. Its members include: grocery shopping lists, debugging sessions, KnowledgeVault architecture decisions, finance reminders, GitHub README updates, and Kubernetes setup notes. These are not semantically related. The category name "Tasks" is not predictive of content.

`Flutter Application Performance.` (note the trailing period — a naming bug) holds 39 notes. The trailing period suggests the LLM extracted a sentence fragment as the category topic.

`Questions - Python` holds 40 notes. This is a question-intent bucket for Python-related questions. Whether it actually contains only Python questions or whether it absorbed general Python study notes via cap_fallback cannot be determined without per-note inspection.

### 1.11 Category Fragmentation

**Reported value:** 0.0% (no singleton categories)

**Grade: Misleading — do not act on this number**

0% fragmentation means every category has multiple notes. This is arithmetically true but causally meaningless: at the 50-category cap with 1055 notes, every category is forced to hold at least 1055/50 ≈ 21 notes on average. The absence of singleton categories is a mathematical consequence of the cap, not evidence of good categorization quality.

### 1.12 Duplicate Categories

**Detected:** 4 duplicate intent-type/topic pairs:
- `Study - PostgreSQL` and `Reference - PostgreSQL`
- `Study - Redis` and `Reference - Redis`
- `Study - Kubernetes` and `Kubernetes` (general intent)
- `Study - AI` and `AI` (general intent)

**Grade: Medium**

These duplicates indicate that identical topics were assigned different intent types by Gemini (study vs reference vs general), causing two category names to be generated for the same conceptual topic. Notes about PostgreSQL are split across two categories, halving the intent arm's ability to retrieve all PostgreSQL notes when a user asks a PostgreSQL question.

### 1.13 Corpus Contamination — Duplicate Notes

**Discovered during diagnostic, not originally planned.**

**Measured:** 100 redundant notes in 20 groups. Each group has exactly 6 copies of the same content. Examples:
- "DBMS normalization: 1NF…" → 6 copies (IDs: 321, 707, 807, 907, 1007, 1107)
- "BM25 is still competitive…" → 6 copies (IDs: 252, 696, 796, 896, 996, 1096)

**Cause:** The dataset generation script (`scripts/data/notes.py`) padded from 952 to 1000 notes by cycling through a 100-note `_EXTRA` list. The extra list was cycled 5× with no deduplication guard.

**Grade: High** (this is an unambiguous data quality defect)

**Measured retrieval impact:** 3.3% of queries (5/152) have 2+ duplicate notes appearing in their top-5 results. These queries are definitionally failing — 2 of 5 retrieval slots are wasted returning identical content. For those queries, effective retrieval capacity is 3 useful results, not 5.

### 1.15 Embedding Quality

**Grade: Good for factual, poor for relational**

The embedding model (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384 dimensions) performs well for factual lookup — MRR 0.832 in the ask benchmark for specific factual questions. It struggles with:

1. **Comparison queries** (recall 0.085): "Redis vs Kafka" needs to simultaneously anchor to two distinct concept clusters. A single query vector cannot be near both clusters at once.
2. **Synthesis queries** (recall 0.119): "Summarize my AI notes" needs to retrieve all AI-related notes, but the query vector naturally gravitates toward the most AI-dense notes rather than the full relevant set.
3. **Ambiguous queries** (recall 0.031): "Notes related to my project" has no anchoring content to embed.

These failures are **architectural properties of single-vector retrieval**, not model-quality issues. Upgrading to a larger embedding model would not fix them. The same failure modes appear in production systems using the largest available embedding models.

### 1.16 Chunking Quality

**Grade: Adequate for current corpus, not tested for documents**

The 1055-note corpus is almost entirely short notes (1–5 sentences). Most notes generate exactly one 500-character chunk, making chunking irrelevant for this corpus. The chunking strategy has not been tested against the PDF/DOCX uploads the system is designed to support. The 50-character overlap is insufficient to bridge sentence boundaries in multi-paragraph documents.

## PART 2 — ROOT CAUSE ANALYSIS

For each weak metric, tracing to the true architectural cause.

---

### Root Cause A — Intent Arm Contributes Nothing

**Symptom:** Intent Hit Rate = 1.3%. ΔMRR = +0.034 (statistically indistinguishable from zero).

**Surface explanation:** Intent classification is wrong.

**Why is it wrong?**

The fast classifier (`extract_query_intent_fast`) uses keyword matching with predefined vocabularies:
```
QUERY_STUDY_KEYWORDS = {"study", "studies", "learn", "learning", "revise", "revision", "practice"}
```

**Why does this fail?**

Users retrieving information from a knowledge base do not use domain-intent vocabulary. They use information-seeking vocabulary:
- "What is X?" → classified "question" (conf=0.55, factor=0)
- "Show me my notes on X" → classified "general" (conf=0.50, factor=0)
- "How does X work?" → classified "question" (conf=0.55, factor=0)

For 50% of queries, `confidence_factor = max(0, (conf - 0.55) / 0.45) = 0`. The entire intent arm produces zero boost. These queries receive exactly the same result as pure semantic retrieval, but at +97ms cost.

**Why does the other 50% also fail?**

Even when `confidence_factor > 0`, the boost is gated by `is_boosted`:
```python
is_boosted = (match.method == "canonical_name") or (
    match.method == "exact_rule" and match.category.actor is not None
)
```

The dominant match method is `vector` (cosine similarity to category embeddings). Vector matches never receive a boost regardless of match quality. The maximum boost achievable under normal conditions is `0.12 × 0.222 = 0.027` — smaller than the typical cosine distance gap between adjacent rank positions.

**Why is intent classification accuracy 14.5% specifically?**

14.5% is a measurement of the classifier against the 152-query benchmark. The benchmark query set is realistic — "What are useful Python tips?", "Show my AI notes", "Document chunking strategy notes." The classifier's keyword vocabulary was designed for note-writing intent (what a user is doing when creating a note), not retrieval intent (what a user wants to find). These are different distributions.

**True root cause:** The fast classifier's keyword vocabulary is calibrated for the wrong task. It predicts note-creation intent; it is used for query-retrieval routing. These are structurally different tasks with different natural language patterns.

---

### Root Cause B — Category System Collapsed at Scale

**Symptom:** 63.3% of notes assigned via `cap_fallback`. Tasks category has 242 notes. Genuine category quality unmeasurable.

**Why?**

`MAX_CATEGORIES_PER_USER = 50` was set when the system had 54 notes. The cap was exceeded at note 155 — the 155th unique note triggered the cap, affecting all subsequent notes.

**Why is 50 wrong for 1055 notes?**

The notes span 19 distinct domains (programming, AI/ML, university, health, travel, finance, meetings, etc.). Even with aggressive topic consolidation, 19 domains × 3–5 sub-topics each = 57–95 natural categories. The 50-category cap cannot represent this diversity.

**Why does cap_fallback make things worse than random assignment?**

`cap_fallback` assigns to the nearest category by embedding similarity. The nearest category for a "Docker networking" note when the 50 categories are already filled might be "Study - Kubernetes" (close in embedding space) rather than "Reference - Docker" (which got created early). The `cap_fallback` note then appears in Kubernetes-related category retrieval but not Docker-related retrieval, damaging recall for Docker queries.

**True root cause:** A hardcoded scalar constant (`50`) was set for a corpus 20× smaller than the current one. It is a configuration error, not an architectural failure.

---

### Root Cause C — Corpus Contamination

**Symptom:** 100 duplicate notes across 20 content groups.

**Why?**

Dataset generation padded from 952 notes to 1000 by cycling a 100-note `_EXTRA` list 5 times. Each cycle produced 5 additional copies of the same 20 notes, totaling 100 duplicates.

**Why does this hurt retrieval?**

Each of the 20 duplicated topics generates 6 identical embeddings in the vector space. When a query is near that topic, all 6 copies appear at nearly identical distance. In a top-5 retrieval, 4–5 slots may be consumed by identical content, leaving 0–1 slots for diverse relevant notes.

Measured direct impact: 5 queries (3.3%) have 2+ duplicates in their top-5. For those queries, effective retrieval diversity is 3 notes, not 5.

**True root cause:** A bug in the dataset generation script — no deduplication guard before writing to the database.

---

### Root Cause D — Benchmark Ground Truth Is Unreliable for Synthesis Queries

**Symptom:** Recall@5 = 0.208 aggregate, but factual lookup Hit Rate = 100%.

**Why?**

Expected notes were generated by keyword matching over 1055 notes. For "Show my Python programming notes," `find_notes(notes, "python")` returns every note containing "python" as a substring, including:
- Note 272: "Career advice: specialize in one area — my T: backend Python/FastAPI" (career note, not Python technical content)
- Note 232: "Heap push O(log n)... Python's heapq is a min-heap" (algorithms note that uses Python syntax)

These notes are legitimately off-topic for "Python programming notes" yet appear in the expected set. The retrieval system correctly excludes them; the benchmark counts their absence as a recall failure.

**True root cause:** The benchmark expected set generation method (keyword matching → any note containing the keyword) is semantically too broad. It treats keyword co-occurrence as relevance equivalence, which it is not.

## PART 4 — IMPROVEMENT ROADMAP

Ranked by ROI. ROI = (expected metric improvement × importance) / (effort × risk).

---

### Improvement 1 — Deduplicate the corpus

**What:** Delete the 100 duplicate notes. Identify all content-identical notes, keep the original (lowest ID), remove the 5 extras.

**Why first:** It is a data defect, not an architectural decision. Every other metric is measured against a contaminated corpus. Measuring anything before deduplication produces unreliable baselines.

**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Effort:** 30 minutes (one SQL DELETE + backfill of affected chunk and embedding references)  
**Expected improvement:**
- Eliminates 3.3% of queries where duplicates consume retrieval slots
- Removes 100 spurious entries from vector search pool
- All downstream metrics become more reliable measurements
**Risk:** Zero — deleting content-identical notes cannot degrade retrieval  
**ROI:** Very High — data quality prerequisite, enables trustworthy evaluation

---

### Improvement 2 — Raise MAX_CATEGORIES_PER_USER from 50 to 200, then backfill

**What:** Change the single constant in `IntentCategoryService`, run `python scripts/backfill_categories.py` to re-categorize the 668 cap_fallback assignments.

**Why second:** The category system is the foundation of the intent arm. The intent arm cannot be evaluated fairly while 63% of notes are in garbage-bin categories. This is a configuration fix, not a feature.

**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Effort:** 1 hour (constant change + backfill script execution)  
**Expected improvement:**
- Tasks category (242 notes) splits into appropriate sub-categories
- Intent arm retrieves from semantically coherent categories
- Expected intent Hit Rate: unknown — this is the experiment that determines whether the intent arm is salvageable
- If intent Hit Rate rises above 50% after proper categorization → intent arm is worth fixing further
- If intent Hit Rate stays below 20% → intent arm is architecturally broken regardless of categories
**Risk:** Low — categories are non-destructive metadata, notes remain intact  
**ROI:** Very High — enables the most important architectural decision (keep or remove intent arm)

---

### Improvement 3 — Fix benchmark ground truth for synthesis and comparison queries

**What:** Replace keyword-based expected note selection with semantic selection for non-factual queries. For each synthesis/comparison query, retrieve the top-K by semantic similarity and manually verify relevance for the top 5–10 results.

**Why third:** The benchmark is currently an unreliable instrument for all query types except factual lookup. Improvement decisions are being made against noise. A trustworthy benchmark is required before any optimization is worth measuring.

**Difficulty:** Medium  
**Effort:** 4–6 hours (semantic retrieval to nominate candidates, manual review of ~200 query-note pairs)  
**Expected improvement:**
- Recall@5 measurement becomes meaningful for all query types
- Provides reliable evidence for or against subsequent improvements
- Enables statistically valid comparison of architectural changes
**Risk:** Low — benchmark data, not production  
**ROI:** High — measurement validity multiplies the value of all other improvements

---

### Improvement 4 — Add HNSW index on all three vector tables

**What:** One Alembic migration adding HNSW indexes (m=16, ef_construction=64) on `embeddings`, `chunk_embeddings`, and `category_embeddings`.

**Why now:** At 1055 notes, sequential scan takes 1.3ms — imperceptible. At 10,000 notes it becomes 12ms. At 50,000 notes it becomes 60ms. The cost to add the index now is 30 minutes. The cost to add it after a performance crisis is an emergency migration under pressure.

**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Effort:** 30 minutes  
**Expected improvement:**
- No quality improvement
- Latency stays near current at current scale
- Latency stays near current as corpus grows to 100,000+ notes
**Risk:** Very Low — HNSW is approximate (98%+ recall at default settings), which is acceptable  
**ROI:** High as preventive infrastructure, Low in current state (needed before scale, not after)

---

### Improvement 5 — Remove the intent arm from the retrieval path

**What:** In `retrieve_hybrid()`, remove the intent arm code path. The method becomes a thin wrapper over the semantic pool query. Keep category assignment at indexing time — it has value for note organization and browsing.

**When:** Only after Improvement 2 (proper categories) and re-evaluation show intent Hit Rate < 30%.

**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Effort:** 2 hours  
**Expected improvement:**
- Retrieval latency: 98.7ms → 1.3ms (−97ms, −98%)
- MRR: −0.034 (if the +0.034 was real signal, which is unconfirmed)
- Simplifies ChunkService by ~130 lines
- Eliminates the confidence_factor, is_boosted, intent_score complexity
**Risk:** Low — ΔMRR is below MDE, meaning the system is already performing as if hybrid = semantic  
**ROI:** High on latency and complexity, depends on Improvement 2 result

---

### Improvement 6 — Expand semantic pool size from 20 to 50

**What:** Change `_SEMANTIC_POOL_MIN = 20` to `_SEMANTIC_POOL_MIN = 50` in `ChunkService`.

**Why:** The intent arm can only rerank notes within the semantic pool. With pool=20 and K=5, notes ranked 21st semantically cannot enter the top-5 regardless of intent signal. Expanding the pool gives the intent arm more material to rerank.

**Note:** This only matters if Improvement 2 shows the intent arm can contribute. If the intent arm is removed, skip this.

**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Effort:** 5 minutes  
**Expected improvement:** +0.02 to +0.05 Recall@5 for queries where relevant notes are in positions 21–50 semantically  
**Risk:** Minimal — retrieval quality cannot degrade; only adds candidates  
**ROI:** Medium — low effort but requires Improvement 2 to validate first

---

### Improvement 7 — Increase context budget from 3000 to 8000 characters

**What:** Change `_MAX_CONTEXT_CHARS = 3000` to `_MAX_CONTEXT_CHARS = 8000` in `ask_service.py`.

**Why:** The current budget sends approximately 750 tokens to a model with a 1,000,000-token context window. This is 99.9% underutilization. For PDF/DOCX uploads (which can produce thousands of words), critical content is truncated before reaching the LLM.

**Difficulty:** Easy  
**Effort:** 5 minutes  
**Expected improvement:** LLM answer quality improves for document-heavy queries. Cannot be measured until Gemini quota resets.  
**Risk:** Low — larger context costs slightly more tokens  
**ROI:** High for answer quality — trivial change, potentially significant answer improvement

### Improvement 8 — Fix the intent classifier vocabulary for retrieval queries

**ROI:** Medium — vocabulary tuning has diminishing returns below 60% accuracy

## PART 5 — WHAT NOT TO TOUCH

These components are working correctly. Do not optimize them.

---

### The LLM Provider Chain

The Gemini → Groq → degraded fallback chain is correctly designed and correctly implemented. Exponential backoff on 429, JSON repair for malformed responses, separate intent and answer chains with justified reasons for excluding Groq from intent extraction — this is production-quality. The rate limit problem during evaluation is a quota issue, not an architecture issue.

### The Graceful Degradation Contract

`AskService` never raises an HTTP 500 from LLM failure. The retrieval-only fallback with accurate source note titles is a genuine engineering achievement. Do not add complexity here.

### The Worker Reliability

`brpoplpush` + inflight queue + startup recovery: 1000/1000 notes processed with 0 failures across 10 batches and ~23 minutes. The reliable at-least-once delivery pattern is correct. Do not change it.

### The Semantic Retrieval Core

`search_similar_notes_with_distance()` using cosine distance is the right approach. For factual lookup — which is the dominant and most important use case — MRR = 0.832, Hit Rate = 100%. The embedding model, distance metric, and query vector encoding are working well. Do not change the embedding model.

### The Deterministic Category Naming

Category names are generated by Python rules, never by LLM. This ensures reproducibility, prevents hallucinated category names, and guarantees that two notes with identical intent metadata always land in the same category. This design decision is correct and should not be reconsidered.

### The Evaluation Framework Structure

The two-level evaluation framework (retrieval metrics + LLM-as-judge) is architecturally sound. The benchmark *data* has quality issues; the benchmark *infrastructure* does not. The `classify_root_cause` taxonomy, `estimate_context_utilisation`, `compute_recall_at_expected`, and debug artifact persistence are all correctly implemented.

## PART 6 — ARCHITECTURE REVIEW

Based strictly on measured benchmark data.

---

### Should Hybrid Retrieval Remain?

**Answer: The architecture should remain, but the implementation must change.**

The measured ΔMRR is +0.034 at 152 queries, below the MDE of 0.067. The intent arm produces no statistically significant improvement.

However, the failure is not in the hybrid concept — it is in three specific compounding defects:
1. Classifier vocabulary mismatch (14.5% accuracy)
2. `is_boosted` gate that blocks boosts for vector matches
3. Category cap creating garbage-bin categories

None of these are fundamental to hybrid retrieval as a concept. If the category system is repaired (Improvement 2), intent accuracy improves above 50%, and the `is_boosted` gate is fixed, hybrid retrieval could produce meaningful ΔMRR. The AI Architecture Review proposed three parameter changes expected to yield +0.04–0.07 ΔMRR on the corrected system.

**Decision: Keep the architecture, run the diagnostic experiment (Improvement 2), then decide.**

The experiment is: raise the category cap, backfill, re-run the benchmark. If ΔMRR remains below MDE, remove the intent arm. If ΔMRR crosses MDE, keep and fix it.

---

### Should Intent Classification Remain in the Query Path?

**Answer: No, not at current accuracy. Temporarily remove it pending the category repair experiment.**

14.5% intent accuracy means the intent arm actively misroutes 85.5% of queries. Even when it guesses correctly, the boost magnitude (max 0.027) is too small to matter. The intent arm currently costs 97ms for no measurable return.

The intent classification system should stay in the **indexing pipeline** (it assigns notes to categories correctly enough for browsing). It should be removed from the **retrieval path** until after the category cap experiment produces a new baseline.

---

### Should Category Generation Be Redesigned?

**Answer: No redesign. One configuration change.**

The category naming logic (deterministic Python rules), the compatibility check before semantic reuse, the intent signature for category embeddings — these are all correctly designed. The single failure is `MAX_CATEGORIES_PER_USER = 50`. Raising it to 200 is a configuration change, not a redesign.

The one genuine design issue — category embedding drift on every assignment — is a secondary concern. After raising the cap, run one evaluation cycle. If category quality remains poor despite the correct cap, then consider a drift guard (only refresh embedding when cosine distance from current embedding exceeds a threshold).

---

### Should the Retrieval Pipeline Change?

**Answer: Yes, one change — add HNSW index. Nothing else until experiments complete.**

The sequential scan must be replaced with an ANN index before corpus size makes it a crisis. Every other retrieval pipeline change depends on the outcome of the category cap experiment.

---

### Should Embedding Strategy Change?

**Answer: No.**

all-MiniLM-L6-v2 achieves MRR = 0.832 for factual lookup — the system's most important query type. It fails for comparison and synthesis queries, but those failures are architectural properties of single-vector bi-encoder retrieval, not model selection failures. Upgrading to a larger model (e.g., `all-mpnet-base-v2`, 768 dimensions) would not fix comparison query failures. The embedding strategy is correct for the use case.

**One necessary addition:** Use chunk embeddings in the hybrid retrieval semantic arm. Currently, `retrieve_hybrid()` uses note-level embeddings while chunk embeddings exist and are maintained but unused. For long documents (PDFs), the note-level embedding averages over the entire document. Chunk-level embeddings enable paragraph-level precision. This change is a one-line swap in `retrieve_hybrid()`.

---

### Should Chunking Strategy Change?

**Answer: Not urgently. Monitor when document uploads become primary input.**

The current 500-char / 50-char overlap strategy is adequate for the dominant corpus (short personal notes). For PDF and DOCX uploads, the chunk size is too small — a 10-page research paper creates 60+ 3-sentence chunks with insufficient context. When document uploads are characterized as a primary use case, increase chunk size to 1000–1500 characters and shift overlap to sentence-boundary aware (not character-boundary).

## PART 7 — NEXT IMPLEMENTATION

### The single highest-ROI improvement is: Deduplicate the corpus, then raise the category cap and run a controlled experiment.

These are two steps but they constitute one experiment — establishing what the system's actual performance is on a clean dataset with proper categorization.

**Why this before everything else:**

Every metric currently measured is contaminated:
1. The corpus has 100 duplicate notes degrading retrieval diversity for affected queries.
2. The category system has 63.3% forced assignments making the intent arm unmeasurable.
3. The benchmark ground truth has keyword-matching artifacts inflating zero-recall counts.

**The architectural decisions that matter most — whether to keep or remove the intent arm — cannot be made until the corpus is clean and the categories are correct.** Making the decision to remove the intent arm now would be acting on evidence that partially reflects category system failure, not intent arm failure. Making the decision to fix the intent arm now would be optimizing a system whose baseline is unknown.

**The experiment:**

1. Delete 100 duplicate notes (30 minutes, zero risk)
2. Raise `MAX_CATEGORIES_PER_USER` from 50 to 200 (5 minutes)
3. Run `python scripts/backfill_categories.py --user-id 1` (re-categorizes 668 cap_fallback notes)
4. Re-run the retrieval benchmark: `python scripts/evaluate.py --user-id 1 --benchmark evaluation_results/benchmarks/retrieval_benchmark.json --output evaluation_results/`

**What the experiment will reveal:**

- **If ΔMRR (Hybrid vs Semantic) crosses 0.069 (MDE):** The category system was the bottleneck. The intent arm works when categories are coherent. Next step: fix the classifier vocabulary.
- **If ΔMRR stays below 0.069:** The intent arm is broken at the classifier level, not the category level. Next step: remove the intent arm from the retrieval path, keep category assignment for browsing only.
- **In both cases:** MRR, Recall, and Hit Rate baselines become trustworthy for the first time.

**Why not start with something else:**

- Fixing the benchmark ground truth (Improvement 3) requires manual review of ~200 query-note pairs — several hours of tedious work. Deduplication takes 30 minutes and eliminates a known defect.
- Adding the HNSW index is correct and should be done, but does not improve any quality metric and does not produce measurement signal.
- Fixing the intent classifier vocabulary (Improvement 8) is wasted effort if Improvement 2 reveals the intent arm is salvageable only with a fully accurate classifier (which keyword-based approaches cannot achieve above ~60%).
- Removing the intent arm is the right decision if the experiment confirms it — but removing it before the experiment means losing the ability to distinguish "intent arm is broken because of poor categories" from "intent arm is broken by design."

**Total experiment cost:** ~2 hours of engineering time, zero production risk, answers the most expensive architectural question in the system.

Do not write code before the experiment results are in.
